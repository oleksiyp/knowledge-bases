# What happened
In an order dated Sept 24, 2026, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín (N.D. Cal.) reversed her own ruling of a year earlier and revived four antitrust counts in WP Engine's suit: monopolization, attempted monopolization and two tying claims. She found it plausible that control of WordPress.org was used to raise competitors' costs and to set unacceptable conditions for access.[^wht-tm-ruling][^sej-antitrust] She also held that Automattic and Matt Mullenweg are "neither owners nor registrants" of the WordPress marks and lack a truly exclusive licence. The WordPress Foundation owns them, so the two cannot assert trademark claims in their own right; Automattic's other counterclaims survive. WP Engine's Computer Fraud and Abuse Act "extortion" claim was dismissed, because the licensing demand had "objective value".[^wht-tm-ruling] A sanctions hearing over allegedly destroyed messages was set for Oct 7, 2026, and a 10-day jury trial for Oct 19, 2027.[^thebuild-oct]

# Why it matters
It is the first ruling to separate the nonprofit that owns an open-source project's trademark from the for-profit company run by the same person. It also keeps alive the theory that control of a project's distribution infrastructure can be an antitrust issue. Both points matter to any vendor-dominated open-source ecosystem.

# Outcome so far
Discovery and sanctions fights continue. The ruling came days after Automattic's board failed to remove Mullenweg as CEO.
