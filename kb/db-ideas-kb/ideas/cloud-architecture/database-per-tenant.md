# Summary
**Niche.** The pattern works, but mostly where databases are almost free to create: SQLite files (Turso, Cloudflare Durable Objects and D1) and scale-to-zero Postgres (Neon, Xata). Turso sells unlimited databases on paid plans and pitches "give every user their own database"[^turso-mt][^turso-dpt]. Developers pick Durable Objects SQLite for per-tenant isolation with 10 GB per object[^penberg-do]. Nile, the most explicit attempt to make Postgres itself tenant-aware, raised an $11.6M seed (announced January 2024) and launched publicly in September 2024. No later funding round is public, and its 2025 output was developer tooling (CLI, MCP server, extension store), not a breakout[^nile-seed][^nile-blog]. The biggest real-world use of "many tiny databases" in 2025–26 is **one per AI agent or per app generated by an AI builder**. Neon's "80% created by agents" is that pattern under another name[^techrepublic-neon].

# The idea
Shared tables with a `tenant_id` column are cheap but leak in every way: noisy neighbors, risky row-level security, painful per-tenant restore or deletion (GDPR), and hard data residency. One database per tenant gives physical isolation, per-tenant backup/restore/move, and simple code. The classic objection is cost and operations: thousands of databases mean thousands of connection pools, migrations and backups. The 2020s bet was that serverless, object-storage-backed engines make each extra database nearly free.

# What succeeded
- **SQLite-shaped platforms.** When a database is a file plus an object-storage backup, creating 10,000 of them is cheap. Turso and Durable Objects made this a normal architecture for SaaS workspaces, chat sessions and per-user state[^turso-dpt][^penberg-do].
- **Per-namespace indexes in search.** turbopuffer and similar systems treat per-tenant namespaces as the unit of isolation and caching. Cursor reportedly runs tens of millions of them[^tpuf].
- **Per-agent databases.** Agents and AI app builders made "one database per generated app" the main driver of serverless Postgres growth[^techrepublic-neon].

# What failed
- **Tenant-aware Postgres as a category.** Nile's tenant virtualization (tenants as first-class objects with per-tenant placement) has not visibly broken out beyond seed stage[^nile-blog]. Mainstream SaaS still uses shared schemas with row-level security, or sharding (Citus, Vitess).
- **Fleet operations.** Running a migration across 50,000 databases, keeping them on one schema version, and running cross-tenant analytics all need tooling most teams lack. They end up exporting everything into a warehouse.
- **Connection and memory overhead in Postgres.** A Postgres database per tenant, unlike a SQLite file, still costs processes and buffers when active. That limits density unless compute scales to zero.

# Why
1. **The cost of an empty database decides adoption.** SQLite files and scale-to-zero Postgres pushed it close to zero. Classic Postgres and MySQL did not.
2. **Most SaaS teams optimize for analytics and simplicity first.** One shared schema makes reporting, billing and migrations easy. Isolation becomes a priority only for regulated or enterprise tiers.
3. **Agents have the ideal tenancy profile.** Many short-lived, independent, mostly idle databases with no cross-tenant queries. That is why the pattern's growth came from AI rather than SaaS.

# Lessons
- Isolation patterns follow the marginal cost of an extra database. Lower it and the architecture changes.
- Plan fleet-wide schema management before you have 10,000 databases.
- "Per-tenant" and "per-agent" are the same pattern. Agents just have no need for cross-tenant joins.
